# Research

## 1. The Problem

1. **Location:** Boys' Hostel 7 (BH-7) Mess, NIT Silchar, housing 3rd- and 4th-year B.Tech students.
2. **Person helped:** Tapon Sahu, Mess Staff and Head Kitchen Coordinator.
3. **Core issue:** Daily quantities of rice, dal, boiled eggs and evening snacks (e.g., Aloo Chop) are set by rough mental estimates.
4. **Consequence (under-prepared):** Student turnout swings between weekdays and weekends, causing emergency mid-shift shortages.
5. **Consequence (over-prepared):** The same swings leave excess or soggy leftovers.

## 2. Two Existing Solutions and Why They Fail for Tapon Sahu

### Digital Mess Management / ERP Apps (e.g., meal pre-booking apps)

- BH-7 runs an open coupon/register entry model; students decide at the last minute whether to eat in the mess or go outside campus, so pre-booking data would not reflect real turnout.
- Tapon Sahu needs a zero-setup, offline tool on the kitchen computer, not an app that requires adoption by 230+ senior students.

### Standard Paper Kitchen Registers

- BH-7 already logs daily raw rations, headcounts and leftovers on paper.
- The numbers stay trapped on separate daily pages, so staff cannot quickly compute multi-day per-student consumption averages or shortage patterns before soaking rice or frying snacks.

## 3. Two Real Sources Read

### Source 1

M. Rajalakshmi and S. Harshavardhini, *Assessment of Food Wastage in Hostel Messes – An Exploratory Qualitative Study*, Journal of Medical Evidence, 2025. (Abstract read.)

1. An exploratory qualitative study of hostel residents on why food is wasted in hostel messes.
2. Causes fell into three groups: student-related, mess-staff-related, and management/warden-related factors.
3. It concludes that wastage occurs even when surplus is produced, pointing to shared inattention rather than a single cause.

### Source 2

V. Shalom, S. Sajan and M. Dela Cruz, *Food Waste Reduction Behaviors Among College Students: An Expanded Theory of Planned Behavior Approach*, AIJR Abstracts (ICETBM 2026), June 2026. (Abstract read.)

1. Applies an Expanded Theory of Planned Behavior to food-waste reduction among students on Indian campuses, with habits as a predictor of attitude, social norms and perceived control.
2. Argues that ingrained consumption and disposal habits can either enable or disable waste-reducing behavior, so intention alone does not translate into action.
3. Suggests interventions targeting habits, rather than intentions or awareness, may achieve more lasting change.
